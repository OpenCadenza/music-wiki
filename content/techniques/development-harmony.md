# Development and harmony cards

## id: `development.transposition-and-sequence`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, sequence, transposition, model, copy
- sources: omt-sentence, omt-sonata-development
- confidence: established
- scope: Material presented as a repeated or sequenced model
- signals: The model's interval and rhythmic pattern remains recognizable while
  sequence units change pitch level or function.
- counterexample: Octave displacement alone does not create motivic development.
- limits: Real, tonal, and free sequences produce different transformations.

Show the model and each copy side by side. State what is invariant and what
changes.

## id: `development.inversion`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, inversion, contour, axis, interval
- sources: umn-canons
- confidence: established
- scope: Melodic or pitch-class inversion
- signals: An explicit axis or interval operation maps the source material to
  actual derived notes.
- counterexample: A contour that merely rises less does not prove inversion.
- limits: Diatonic inversion, chromatic inversion, and tonal imitation differ.

Define the operation and mark at least the first and structurally important
pitches in source and derivative.

## id: `development.augmentation-and-diminution`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, augmentation, diminution, duration, rhythm
- sources: umn-canons
- confidence: established
- scope: Duration-based transformation
- signals: The interval or rhythmic pattern survives while the duration ratio
  changes by a stated factor.
- counterexample: Faster sixteenth-note accompaniment under an unchanged theme
  is not thematic diminution.
- limits: Proportional and free augmentation are distinct.

Compute source and derived durations. Name the transformed material itself.

## id: `development.fragmentation`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, fragmentation, continuation, phrase rhythm
- sources: omt-sentence, omt-sonata-development
- confidence: established
- scope: Continuation or developmental intensification
- signals: A larger melodic unit is broken into shorter recognizable units.
- counterexample: Any short motive is not necessarily fragmentation; the larger
  source unit and the shortening relationship must be shown.
- limits: Repetition can fragment without sequence.

Label unit lengths and the retained characteristic figure. Show how the phrase
loses rhetorical size.

## id: `development.liquidation`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, liquidation, conventional, cadence
- sources: omt-sentence
- confidence: established
- scope: Continuation leading toward cadence
- signals: Characteristic material is progressively removed and replaced by
  conventional scale, arpeggio, or cadential motion.
- counterexample: Simply adding scales beside the untouched theme does not
  liquidate that theme.
- limits: The concept belongs primarily to classical phrase analysis.

Mark characteristic figures that disappear. Check the cadence that follows.

## id: `development.role-exchange`

- type: procedure
- facets: development, texture, role, melody, bass, voice exchange
- sources: project-workflow
- confidence: provisional
- scope: Requests for bass leadership, voice exchange, or new-texture variation
- signals: The transferred material is recognizable in the new voice while old
  roles release register, accent, articulation, or continuity.
- counterexample: A bass playing the old accompaniment more loudly is not a
  successful role exchange.
- limits: Role exchange is an interpretive description unless the task specifies
  it; static textures can deliberately retain the same distribution.

Use three checks: material identity, structural prominence, and other voices'
actual yielding.

## id: `harmony.functions`

- type: concept
- facets: harmony, function, tonic, subdominant, dominant, context
- sources: omt-harmonic-functions
- confidence: established
- scope: Common-practice tonal harmony
- signals: A chord is interpreted from its scale degrees, surrounding chords,
  and phrase position.
- counterexample: Chord quality or root alone does not define function in every
  style.
- limits: Pop, modal, post-tonal, and later chromatic music need their own models.

Annotate Roman numeral and functional role. Do not apply common-practice
tendencies to a style that does not organize harmony that way.

## id: `harmony.cadences`

- type: fact
- facets: harmony, cadence, PAC, IAC, HC, arrival
- sources: omt-cadences
- confidence: established
- scope: Classical cadence analysis
- signals: Cadence type is checked from bass scale degree, melodic arrival, and
  harmonic function at the formal boundary.
- counterexample: A melodic scale-degree gesture alone can suggest but not prove
  a complete cadence.
- limits: Modern styles and phrase elision require context.

Record chord progression, bass motion, top-voice pitch, key, and formal unit.

## id: `harmony.prolongation`

- type: concept
- facets: harmony, prolongation, function, inversion, pedal
- sources: omt-prolongation
- confidence: established
- scope: Tonal phrases understood hierarchically
- signals: Multiple local chords sustain a broader function through common tone,
  changed bass, inversion, or contrapuntal motion.
- counterexample: Every consecutive chord sharing a broad family is not a
  meaningful prolongation.
- limits: Analytical depth depends on duration and formal function.

Note the prolonged function and the technique. Do not collapse the concrete
voice-leading into a label.

## id: `harmony.modulation`

- type: procedure
- facets: harmony, modulation, pivot, tonicization, cadence, key
- sources: omt-modulation
- confidence: established
- scope: Common-practice tonal modulation
- signals: A new key is established strongly enough to include a cadence or
  articulated key region; a pivot chord is diatonic to both keys.
- counterexample: A brief applied dominant is tonicization, not automatically a
  modulation.
- limits: Direct or phrase modulation can omit a pivot.

State old key, new key, pivot or direct move, confirming cadence, and source
location.

## id: `harmony.dissonance-and-suspension`

- type: procedure
- facets: harmony, dissonance, suspension, preparation, resolution
- sources: omt-fourth-species, omt-cadences
- confidence: established
- scope: Tonal satb, keyboard, and counterpoint analysis
- signals: Dissonance is classed by function and checked for preparation,
  rhythm, and resolution across actual voices.
- counterexample: A chordal seventh can be valid without following species
  suspension mechanics.
- limits: Interval friction and harmonic dissonance are different concepts.

Identify both the vertical interval and the broader harmonic tone role.

## id: `harmony.planing-and-modern-parallelism`

- type: concept
- facets: harmony, modern, parallelism, planing, context
- sources: project-workflow
- confidence: interpretive
- scope: Non-functional or color-driven parallelism in modern tonal music
- signals: The repeated sonority, bass trajectory, phrase position, and intended
  color are shown from the score.
- counterexample: Modern style does not make every accidental parallel harmless.
- limits: The card cannot prove that a specific Ravel passage licenses a broad
  imitation.

Describe the chord type and voice motion. Ask whether the style treats the line
as an independent voice, a fused sonority, or an intentional intensification.

## id: `orchestration.climax-arrival`

- type: procedure
- facets: orchestration, climax, preparation, arrival, release, dynamics
- sources: project-workflow
- confidence: provisional
- scope: Directed dramatic passages
- signals: Harmonic preparation, rhythmic acceleration, register expansion,
  texture, timbral change, and the arrival point form a chain; release or
  continuation is planned after the peak.
- counterexample: Adding `ff` at the largest chord does not create a prepared
  climax.
- limits: Static or anti-climactic music can deliberately refuse this model.

Write the peak coordinate and the conditions that distinguish it from the bars
before and after.
